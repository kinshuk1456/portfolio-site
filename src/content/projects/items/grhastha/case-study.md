## The problem

Performing a puja at home the right way asks three things of you at the same time: knowing **when** it should happen, knowing **how** to actually do it, and knowing **what the words mean**. For a lot of households that knowledge is scattered — half-remembered, inconsistent online, or quietly lost between generations. And the apps that promise to help often wrap something sacred in logins, subscriptions, and merchandising that feel wrong for the moment they're meant to serve.

## Context

Grhastha is a family-led, non-profit-oriented effort. That orientation isn't a footnote — it's the constraint that shapes the whole product. There's no subscription model and no login, on purpose, because the goal is to preserve access and authenticity rather than convert devotion into a funnel.

## The design principles

A few rules that everything else answers to:

- **No login.** You shouldn't have to make an account to pray.
- **Calm, not SaaS.** The interface should feel like a sacred calendar or almanac, not a dashboard.
- **Authentic, not gamified.** No streaks, no badges, no points on something sacred.
- **A Madhubani-inspired Ganesh** as the visual anchor, so it reads as devotional and rooted, not generic.
- **Onboard once.** First-run guidance the first time; after that you land straight in the home experience — no "Coming Soon" merchandising in the way.

## What it is: three pillars

**Know When** — location-aware puja timing and muhurta, so the *when* is right for where you actually are.

**Know How** — step-by-step guidance through the puja, including preparation, the materials checklist, and workable substitutes when you don't have something on hand.

**Know the Words** — the Sanskrit mantras with audio, transliteration, and their meanings, so you're not just sounding out syllables but understanding what you're saying.

The initial focus is **Ganesh Chaturthi**, with other festivals to follow — deep on one occasion first, rather than shallow across many.

## The design challenge

The hard part isn't features, it's restraint. Cultural and religious products fail when they either dilute the tradition to fit a familiar app pattern, or bury the user in complexity in the name of authenticity. Grhastha's whole design bet is that you can **reduce the complexity without reducing the authenticity** — make it easy to do it *properly*, which is a very different goal from making it easy.

## What I learned

This is the clearest example I have of designing technology around cultural sensitivity: letting the values (access, authenticity, calm) set the product rules, instead of importing SaaS defaults and hoping they fit. Getting the *substitutes* feature right — "you can use this instead" — did more for real households than any amount of polish would have.
